Restaurant Menu Design: 12 Tips for a Menu That Sells

By Isaiah Lewis, Zev DOD · November 23, 2026 · 3 min read

Your menu is the one piece of marketing every customer reads. A clear, well-organized menu helps guests order faster, helps staff answer fewer questions, and points people toward the dishes you most want to sell. Here are twelve practical tips.

Layout and organization

1. Group items the way people decide. Most guests choose a category first (plates, sides, drinks), then an item. Use clear section headings in a logical order: starters, mains, sides, desserts, drinks.

2. Keep each section short. Long lists make decisions harder. If a section has more than about seven items, consider splitting it or trimming slow sellers.

3. Give your best dishes room. Put a signature item in a box, give it a short description, or place it at the top of its section. One or two highlights per page is plenty. Highlight everything and nothing stands out.

4. Leave white space. Crowded menus feel cheap and are hard to scan in dim light. Margins and spacing between sections make the whole menu easier to read.

Prices

5. Line prices up neatly, without dotted leaders. Long dotted lines pull the eye straight to the price column. Put the price at the end of the description instead.

6. Keep the formatting consistent. Pick one style ("14" or "$14" or "14.00") and use it everywhere.

7. Only print prices you're confident in. If prices change often, a phone-friendly digital menu is easier to update than a reprint.

Words and type

8. Write short, specific descriptions. "Smothered chicken, slow-cooked gravy, rice" tells guests more than "our famous chicken." Name the ingredients people care about.

9. Use no more than two fonts. One for headings, one for items. Make sure the item font is readable at arm's length. Script fonts look nice in a logo but are hard to read in a list.

10. Check contrast. Light gray text on a cream background disappears under restaurant lighting. Dark text on a light background is the safest choice.

Photos, print and digital

11. Use photos carefully. One or two great photos of real dishes help. Lots of small, dim photos make a menu look busy. If you use photos, they should be of your food.

12. Plan for both print and phone. Many guests check your menu on their phone before they visit. Ask your designer for:

  • A print-ready PDF at the right size, with a bleed if your printer asks for one.
  • A phone-friendly image or page sized for scrolling, for Google, Instagram and your website.

Common menu sizes

Format Typical use
8.5 × 11 in, one page Quick-service, food trucks, counters
8.5 × 14 in (legal) Larger single-page menus
Two-sided More items without a booklet
Tri-fold Takeout and to-go menus

Ask your print shop what paper weight and finish they recommend. A heavier, matte stock holds up better at the table.

Before you print: a quick checklist

  • Every price and item name double-checked by someone who didn't type them
  • Hours, address, phone or ordering link included
  • Allergen or spice notes where needed
  • Readable from about three feet away
  • Matches your sign, social graphics and website
